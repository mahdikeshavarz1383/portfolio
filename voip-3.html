VoIP

Voice over IP — how phone calls travel over the same networks as everything else, and what tends to break along the way.

What it is

A quick technical overview — replace or expand with your own explanation as you learn more.

VoIP (Voice over Internet Protocol) converts voice into digital packets and sends them over an IP network instead of the traditional analog phone system. This is what lets office phone systems, apps like softphones, and services like Telegram calls all work over a regular internet connection.

A VoIP call typically involves two separate jobs: signaling (setting up, managing, and tearing down the call) and media (the actual audio stream). These are handled by different protocols, which is exactly why VoIP troubleshooting often splits into "the call won't connect" problems versus "the call connects but sounds bad" problems.

  • SIPSession Initiation Protocol — handles call setup, teardown, and registration between a phone/softphone and a server.
  • REGISTERThe SIP method a device uses to tell the server "I'm online and reachable at this address." Registration failures are one of the most common VoIP issues.
  • RTPReal-time Transport Protocol — carries the actual audio once the call is set up.
  • CodecHow audio is compressed for transmission (e.g. G.711, Opus). Codec mismatches cause one-way or no audio.
  • NATNetwork Address Translation — the single most common source of VoIP headaches, since it can break how a device's real address is reported inside SIP/RTP.
